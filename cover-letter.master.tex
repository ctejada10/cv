% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
% A one-page letter in five blocks (250--350 words), with a bank of
% proof paragraphs you reuse and slots you must write fresh each time.
%
%   1. Hook        45--65 words  CUSTOM  the role, and the one true sentence
%                                        about why this posting and not another
%   2. Proof 1     70--90 words  BANK + one custom sentence tying it to them
%   3. Proof 2     60--80 words  BANK + one transition sentence
%   4. Why them    40--60 words  CUSTOM  where you have actually touched their
%                                        work, and the problem you'd start on
%   5. Close       20--35 words  BANK (fill its slot)
%
% THE RULE THAT MATTERS MOST: the letter must carry what the resume cannot.
% The resume already says what you built and what it scored. The letter says
% what the decision was, what surprised you, what you did when it broke, what
% you enjoyed. A paragraph that only restates a bullet in longer sentences is
% wasted paper (Green, Ask a Manager: a letter should not be "your resume in
% different words"). Her rewrite that works reads "I probably was the first to
% read the entire manual, which made me 'the P560 person'" -- a small, concrete,
% slightly odd fact that no one else could write.
%
% Why this shape: since AI writing tools arrived, letters that echo a posting's
% keywords stopped predicting callbacks (Cui, Dias & Ye 2025; Galdin & Silbert
% 2025), and Harvard's 2026 guidance warns that recruiters now spot unedited AI
% by its generic, over-formal register. So specificity is the whole strategy.
%
% Banned from every block, because they are what the generated letters do:
%   * restating the posting's requirements back at them
%   * praise of the company that could be copied into any other letter
%     ("you ship at scale and still publish", "industry leader")
%   * claiming you exceed the requirements
%   * a paragraph-closing aphorism, and the em-dash pivot that sets it up
%   * "I am writing to express my interest"; weak verbs (helped with, assisted)
%   * announcing what you do NOT have ("I don't have a clean answer to that")
%
% BLOCK 4 in particular is not a compliment. It is the place where you have
% actually touched their work: a product you use, a paper you read, a tool you
% built on, a bug you hit, a dataset you pulled. If nothing like that is true,
% cut the block rather than fill it with admiration.
%
% Build:
%   scripts/build.sh master  -> build/master/cover-letter-master.pdf
%                               (skeleton + every bank paragraph)
%   scripts/build.sh app applications/<file>.tex -> Carlos-Tejada-Cover-Letter.pdf
% =====================================================================

\letterpara{proof-nc-pipeline}{ds,de,mle}{At \FactNCShort, a \FactNCAbout, I built the Azure/PySpark pipeline that turned billions of vehicle-telemetry records a month into the trip-level tables our predictive models ran on, and I was the technical contact for the \FactNCTeams{} sales, operations and business-development teams that used its output. Because our clients' contracts required it, I also put role-based access control and audit logging on sensitive data across \FactNCClients{} client systems.}

\letterpara{proof-nc-nlp}{ds,mle}{For a government client at \FactNCShort{} I built and deployed a \FactNCLanguages-language document classifier (scikit-learn, TensorFlow) that replaced manual triage on tens of thousands of documents a month at \FactNCFone{} F1. The documents drifted, so holding that number took periodic retraining and versioned deployment, not one good evaluation at launch.}

\letterpara{proof-nc-tools}{ds,de,uxr}{I also automated the reporting behind \FactNCToolTeams{} teams' weekly work with Python and SQL services and Power BI dashboards. That cut reporting overhead by \FactNCOverhead{} and gave non-technical colleagues self-service access to data that used to take a request to engineering.}

\letterpara{proof-uw-rag}{ds,mle,rs}{At the \FactUWOrg{} I built a retrieval-augmented companion that describes 3D models to blind users. The engineering went into retrieval rather than prompting: the system scores every cross-section of a model, selects the most informative, and the LLM only describes slices it has been handed. Without it the model invents geometry that is not there, which someone reading a braille display cannot catch.}
% ASK CARLOS: one real instance -- a description the model invented, or a slice that
% scored well and turned out useless. A named failure beats a stated failure mode.

\letterpara{proof-uw-study}{ds,rs,uxr}{This summer I led a qualitative study with \FactUWStudyN{} blind and low-vision participants, who compared 3D-printed objects with virtual models on a refreshable braille display while thinking aloud. Because I had instrumented every command in the system, we could pair the thematic analysis with how each person actually explored: which views they chose, how deep they sliced, and how their strategy changed between tasks. I'm the lead author on the resulting submission.}

\letterpara{proof-uw-system}{mle,rs,uxr}{I'm the lead developer of a system that lets blind and low-vision people explore and edit 3D models by touch. A Python backend slices the model on demand and streams each cross-section to refreshable pin displays (APH Monarch, Dot Pad) from a screen-reader-accessible web app that runs in Chrome with nothing to install. I designed its interaction model with a DeafBlind co-designer, who is a co-author on the work.}

\letterpara{proof-aarhus-llm}{mle,rs}{At \FactAUOrg{} I built two LLM tools: a design assistant that turns natural-language prompts into CAD for interactive, 3D-printable devices, and a query tool that let non-technical collaborators ask questions of research datasets in plain language. In both, most of the work was validating what the model produced against the constraints of the workflow it fed: geometry that has to be printable, answers that have to be checkable against the data.}

\letterpara{proof-capstone-leak}{ds,mle,de,rs}{For my M.Sc. capstone I built a classifier that identified materials from how they sounded, so a laser cutter could load the right settings by itself. Five-fold cross-validation said \FactKoWLeak. Then it failed on recordings it hadn't seen. Samples from a single microphone placement had landed on both sides of the split, so the model had learned the placement rather than the material. I rebuilt the evaluation on held-out placements, and the honest number was \FactKoWAccuracy.}

\letterpara{proof-phd-experiments}{ds,rs,uxr}{During my Ph.D. I invented and evaluated \FactFirstAuthor{} sensing techniques for 3D-printed and flexible objects, and each design came out of measurement rather than intuition: I swept chamber geometry, outlet spacing and tube dimensions against ground truth and let the data choose the parameters. The classifiers on top reached \FactPhDAccuracy{} accuracy with no per-user calibration, and \FactPhDBlowAccuracy{} cross-validated on \FactPhDSamples{} labeled samples from \FactPhDDataParticipants{} participants. The work produced \FactFirstAuthor{} first-author papers at CHI, UIST, ISS and GI.}

\letterpara{proof-mentoring}{mle,rs,uxr}{I also like making other people's work go faster. At the \FactUWOrg{} I mentor \FactUWMentees{} undergraduate researchers building view-selection and slice-scoring pipelines for our system, and during my Ph.D. I supervised \FactPhDTheses{} master's theses, one of which became a TEI paper.}
